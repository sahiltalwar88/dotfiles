# Open the picture, then say what you think he means

Two rules, both of which Sahil has had to ask for more than once. He named it a
recurring problem on 2026-09-19:

> "please also save a memory to ALWAYS ACTUALLY OPEN THE PICTURES I GIVE YOU and
> ALWAYS give me a summary of what you think i'm trying to get at, to verify your
> understanding. this is a recurring problem"

## 1. Open the image. Actually look at it.

When he sends a screenshot, look at the picture. Not the `?perf` panel text in the
corner, not the URL in the address bar, not a measurement taken offline that ought
to describe the same thing. Those are all ways of talking about the frame without
seeing it, and each time I have substituted one for the other I have given him a
confident wrong answer.

The failure has a signature: an argument of the form "my harness says the geometry
is flat, therefore what you are seeing is X." Offline measurement describes the
scene as built; his screenshot shows the scene as rendered, at his zoom, on his
display. When the two disagree, the screenshot is right and the measurement is
answering a different question. On the Saturn rings I insisted twice that rocks
were in the ring plane because build-time instance matrices said so, while he could
plainly see whole rocks standing clear of the disc — and had to tell me to open the
images.

If he sends a crop, note that it is magnified: a half-unit offset invisible in a
full frame is obvious in a zoom, so "too small to see" is not an argument against
what he is looking at.

## 2. Say what you think he is asking for, before you act on it

Every time he points at something, open the reply with a one-line statement of
what you understand the target to be and what outcome he wants — so he can correct
it in a word instead of after the work. He asks for this explicitly and it is
cheap: a sentence at the top, not a section.

It matters most when the request is deictic — "these", "this one", "the floaters",
a green circle on a crop. Those words carry no coordinates, and the cost of
guessing wrong is a round of work aimed at the wrong object. Twice in one evening
I built and measured the wrong thing because I inferred the referent instead of
naming it back to him.
