# Lead with what he owned, not how he improved it

While editing his LinkedIn profile on 2026-09-21, I ranked two process-and-method
bullets as the strongest on his page: an AI-native software development lifecycle
transformation, and an engineering governance framework built on DORA/SPACE
metrics. Sahil pushed back on both with the same question:

> "do you think that's really a better bullet than the one that states that i
> owned engineering for the product that earned over 90% of redshelf's revenue,
> over $35M?"

and

> "do you really think that's better than running an org of 25, maintaining
> 99.99% availability for over 8M MAUs generating over 20M requests per day?"

He is right, and the principle generalizes. In anything that positions him
professionally — LinkedIn bullets, the résumé, the casefile sections on the
`/consulting` site — **the ordering is scope and ownership first, method
second.** Revenue owned, organization size, user counts, and system scale are
the credential; process improvements are supporting evidence for it. This holds
for both of his audiences: an executive search screens on scope, and a
consulting buyer finds "he ran the thing that earned the money" more persuasive
than "he knows the right metrics framework."

Method bullets still belong high in the list, because they are what
differentiates him from other candidates with similar scope, but they do not
take the first slot in a role's bullet list.

A related habit worth keeping: when drafting his bullets, do not silently invent
numbers he has not given. Write the bullet without the figure and list the
missing figures separately for him to fill in.
