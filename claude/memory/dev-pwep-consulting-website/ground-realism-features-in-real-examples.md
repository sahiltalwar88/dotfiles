# Be able to point at the real thing

When a change to the pwep world is justified as realism, Sahil expects it to correspond to
something that actually exists, and he will ask. On a facade feature he asked plainly:
**"can you find me an example of a building that has this in real life? i'm not sure i
understand what this is supposed to represent; it basically seems to invert the colors of
the window and the wall rather than representing an actual band of steel somewhere."**

He was right, and the feature was removed. The underlying thing (a spandrel panel — the
opaque panel that hides the floor slab between one storey's glass and the next) is real,
but it belongs inside a glass curtain wall among dark glass and bright mullions. Applied
to a tower whose wall is pale stone, darkening the band between window rows only swapped
which stripe was dark. The idea was real; its application to that geometry was not, and no
photograph would have shown what had been built.

So before building a realism feature:

- Find the reference first — a photograph, a named building, a documented detail — and
  check that what you intend to draw is what the reference actually shows ON THE GEOMETRY
  YOU HAVE. A detail that is true of a glass tower can be false on a masonry one.
- Put the reference in the reply, with a link. He engages with the example, not the
  assertion, and this is the same instinct behind his standing "always research when i'm
  asking you to do something new".
- If no reference can be found, DO NOT drop it on your own judgement. His correction, in
  his words: "don't drop it, flag it for me, explain the idea behind it, describe the
  effect you think it will have, and then ask me if you should proceed or not." So say
  plainly that you could not find a real example, explain what the idea was meant to
  represent, say what you expect it would look like in the frame, and ask. The decision is
  his; an unreferenced idea may still be one he wants, and silently deleting it takes that
  choice away from him.

This is close to, but distinct from, researching existing implementations before a
rethink: that one is about how other people solved the problem in code, this one is about
whether the thing being depicted exists in the world.
